How Deckboy compares

Including the jobs where something else is the better answer.

Deckboy is a cue-based video playback deck: a playlist of clips that go to air when the operator says so, out of a machine, into a screen. These are the tools it actually sits beside on a show. It is not trying to be all of them, and for several jobs one of them is plainly the right call. This page says which is which, because a comparison that flatters itself throughout is an advertisement wearing a lab coat.

The thing the table is really saying

Four of the five are macOS only, and the fifth is Windows only. Mitti, PlaybackPro, QLab and Millumin do not run on a PC at all; vMix does not run on a Mac. If the machine going to the venue is not the machine the software wants, that is the end of the conversation before features are even discussed.

The other column is money. Mitti, PlaybackPro, Millumin and vMix are paid software, per machine, and a second playback machine means a second licence — which is the moment most people discover what their redundancy plan costs. QLab is the fair exception: it has a genuinely free tier that includes video cues, and charges for the advanced features on top.

Deckboy is free. Not a trial, not a watermark, not a feature-limited tier with the useful half behind a licence server: the whole application, on all three operating systems, under the GPL. The spare machine in the flight case can run it too, and so can the one you are reading this on.

What none of the others give you

Not "does it better" — does not have. Every item here is in the free download, on all three platforms:

  • Your own VST3 plugins, inside a cue. The reverb you already own or the channel strip your mix is built around sits in that cue's chain beside Deckboy's own effects, with the plugin's controls on the rows beneath it and its settings saved in the show. A plugin that cannot keep up with its block is taken out and reported, rather than clicking through the PA.
  • Any window on the machine, as a cue. A score bug, a timer, a rehearsal tool, the spreadsheet somebody insists on — at the window's own resolution whatever the display scaling is, following it as it moves, with anything in front of it kept out of shot, so the operator carries on working on the same box. How that works.
  • A web page as a cue, rendered inside the programme rather than in a browser window you have to keep on top.
  • Effects that are instruments rather than filters: schlieren imaging, Chladni figures, a real wave equation, crystal grain growth, retinal persistence, structure-tensor grain flow — and two that cross between picture and sound in both directions.
  • A test-pattern generator and a screen-check tool in the same application that plays the show, so the wall can be proved before anyone arrives.
  • It runs with no sound card at all — a rack PC feeding a video wall opens, plays and puts a picture out.
  • One show file on Windows, macOS and Linux. The others each run on one operating system.

Where Deckboy tends to fit

  • The job is playing video cues, reliably, on the night. A playlist of clips with trim, fades, loops and hold-last-frame, hardware-accelerated decode, and a cue that knows how long it has left, and thirteen cue-to-cue transition styles — cut, crossfade, dips, four pushes, four wipes and an iris. If a client sends a file that will not play, it converts in place without leaving the application; if they send one mastered too quietly, it normalises to EBU R128. That is the day job, and everything below is what happens after it is handled.
  • The show machine is a PC, or a mixture. "QLab for Windows" and "Mitti for Windows" are common searches for a reason. Deckboy runs natively on Windows, macOS and Linux from the same show file.
  • The signal has to leave in more than one shape. NDI, SRT, RTMP, Blackmagic SDI and SMPTE ST 2110 can run at once, so the playback machine often solves the conversion problem on the day as well.
  • It has to be driven from a surface. A Stream Deck plugin and a Bitfocus Companion module, plus OSC, MIDI, Art-Net and LTC timecode.
  • Slides are part of the show. PDF, PowerPoint and Keynote import as one cue per slide with speaker notes, and presenter and prompter views are output types of their own. Most cue players do not handle slides at all.
  • Something on the machine has to go to air. A window on the show PC can be a cue — a score bug, a timer, a rehearsal tool, the spreadsheet somebody insists on — at the window’s own resolution whatever the display scaling is, following it as it moves, with anything in front of it kept out of shot, so the operator can carry on working on the same box. A web page can be a cue too, rendered inside the programme rather than in a browser window you have to keep on top. Most cue players send you to a capture card or a second machine for either.
  • The sound needs to follow the picture. Five of the twenty-two per-cue audio effects read the frame they are playing under — its brightness, where it sits in the raster, how long it has left. A plug-in in a desk receives samples and nothing else, so it cannot. Two effects cross the other way as well: databend plays the picture through an audio chain and paints what comes back, and audioprint draws the sound the deck has just played through the picture.
  • Budget is zero. GPL-3.0, and nothing phones home.

Where one of the others is the better answer

This section is here on purpose. Recommending the wrong tool wastes a show day.

  • A Mac-only shop that wants the most refined cue player — buy Mitti. It is focused, mature and beautifully made, and being narrower than Deckboy is the point of it rather than a shortcoming. The moment one machine in the rack is a PC, though, the question answers itself.
  • Corporate events where the operator already knows the tool — PlaybackPro. Years of rooms behind it, and familiarity on a show day is worth more than a feature list.
  • Audio-led theatre cueing on a Mac — QLab. A deep audio cue model and lighting cues in the same stack. Deckboy is video-first and has no lighting cues.
  • Projection mapping and live visuals — Millumin. Deckboy has warp, keystone and edge blend per output, which covers ordinary multi-projector work, but it is not a mapping suite.
  • Multi-camera switching and streaming a programme — vMix. Deckboy is a source and a player, not a switcher. The two pair well: Deckboy out over NDI, vMix taking it as an input.

Honest caveats

  • The builds are not code-signed. Signing needs paid Apple and Microsoft developer accounts that the project has not bought, so macOS and Windows will warn about an unknown developer. The README explains the one-time steps.
  • It is a young project compared with everything else on this page, and it has not survived the number of show days those tools have. Mitti and PlaybackPro have been quietly not-failing in ballrooms for years, which is a form of credential no feature list can forge.
  • It does an unusual number of things, and that is deliberate: a show machine that can also prove a screen, convert a file a client sent in the wrong codec and generate a test card saves a van and an afternoon. If you want one job done and nothing else on the screen, a narrower tool is a fair choice.
