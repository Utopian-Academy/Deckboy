Where latency
hides

Four numbers, not one. And only one of them is yours to spend.

Somebody asks how much delay there is between the camera and the screen, and the honest answer is a question: delay through what? A live chain has at least four places that cost time, they are measured in different units by different vendors, and the largest of them is usually the one nobody owns.

At 59.94 fields a second, one frame is 16.683 ms. At 50, it is 20 ms. Every number below is quoted in frames where the cause is structural and in milliseconds where it is not, because a chain quoted entirely in milliseconds hides which parts scale with frame rate and which do not.

1. Capture: one frame, and no argument

A sensor has to finish reading out before there is a frame to hand anybody. That is one frame, it is not a software problem, and no product can sell you out of it. Rolling-shutter sensors deliver the top of the image earlier than the bottom, which helps the top line and nothing else.

What varies is what happens immediately after: a camera doing noise reduction or stabilisation in-body may hold another frame or two to do it. If a camera has a “low latency” or “direct” mode, that is usually what it is turning off.

Budget: 1 frame, plus whatever the camera's processing is set to.

2. Encode: the one you actually choose

This is the only stage where the decision is yours, and it is where the range is widest.

  • Intra-only codecs (uncompressed, JPEG XS, ProRes, the full NDI codec) encode each frame without reference to any other, so the encoder can emit a frame as soon as it has one. About 1 frame.
  • Long-GOP codecs (H.264, HEVC — so NDI HX, SRT and RTMP in normal use) buffer to build a group of pictures. How long depends on the GOP length and whether B-frames are on. Typically 2 to 8 frames, or roughly 33–133 ms at 59.94.

The trap is that nothing on the front panel tells you which one a box has chosen. Two encoders both advertised as “low latency H.264” can differ by five frames because one has B-frames enabled and the other does not. If the specification sheet does not say intra-only or give a GOP length, assume long-GOP and measure it.

Budget: 1 frame if intra-only, 2–8 if not.

3. Transport: smaller than you think, unless it is Wi-Fi

Moving bits across a switch is the cheapest part of the chain, and it is routinely blamed for the other three.

  • SDI is a continuous serial stream. Effectively zero — measured in lines, not frames.
  • SMPTE ST 2110 on a properly provisioned network, with senders locked to a PTP grandmaster (SMPTE 2059-2, sub-microsecond), is under a millisecond. It is not compressed, which is the entire point of it — and the reason the conversation about 2110 is always about the switch. One 1080p59.94 10-bit 4:2:2 flow is 2.49 Gb/s (1920 × 1080 × 20 bits × 59.94). The same raster at UHD is 9.95 Gb/s. Two of those do not fit in a 10-gig port.
  • SRT deliberately adds a buffer so it can retransmit lost packets. The usual guidance is a latency setting of 2 to 4× the round-trip time, and it is honest about it — the number is in the configuration, where you can read it.
  • Wi-Fi is not a transport for live video. Its latency is not a number, it is a distribution, and the tail is where your show lives.

Budget: under 1 ms wired. For SRT, whatever you set, because you set it.

4. Display: the one that actually hurts

A projector or a large-format display is a computer. It deinterlaces, scales, applies keystone correction, does frame-rate conversion and runs whatever picture processing the factory left on. Each of those can cost a frame.

Three things make this the worst stage:

  • It is rarely in the specification sheet. Consumer displays publish input lag because gamers demand it; projectors sold into events often do not.
  • It changes when somebody touches the picture menu. Switching from a “cinema” preset to a “bright” one can change the processing path and therefore the delay.
  • It is not your equipment. It belongs to the venue, and it was configured by somebody who has left.

Where a display exposes a game, PC or low-latency mode, that mode is the processing being switched off, and it is usually worth more than any other single change in the chain.

Budget: 1 to 3 frames, up to about 50 ms, unmeasured until you measure it.

For reference: lip-sync error becomes noticeable to most viewers at around 45 ms of audio lead and roughly 125 ms of audio lag (ITU-R BT.1359). A presenter watching themselves on a delayed relay screen notices far sooner than that, which is why IMAG delay is the complaint that reaches you first.

How to measure it, with things you already have

All of this is guesswork until you measure the chain you actually have. The method needs no test equipment:

  1. Put a running millisecond timer on a screen at the start of the chain — a phone stopwatch is fine, so is a timecode window burn.
  2. Point the camera at it, and put the output display beside it, in the same shot.
  3. Photograph both with a second camera, at a fast shutter. A phone at 1/1000 s will do.
  4. The difference between the two readings is your end-to-end latency.

Do this once per stage as well as end to end — camera to monitor, then add the encoder, then add the display — and you will find the frame nobody knew about. It is almost never where the argument said it was.

For audio against video specifically, a flash-and-click beacon (a white frame with a single click on the same frame) recorded off the end of the chain and examined in any audio editor gives you the A/V offset directly, in samples.

The one-line version: capture costs a frame and you cannot argue with it, encoding is the only choice you actually make, transport is innocent until it is wireless, and the projector is lying to you.

Where this came from

These are the numbers behind Deckboy, a free, open-source cue-based video playback deck for Windows, macOS and Linux. It sits at the encode stage of the chain above: NDI, SRT, RTMP, Blackmagic SDI and SMPTE ST 2110 out of one machine, several at once, with a built-in A/V delay beacon for exactly the measurement described here.
